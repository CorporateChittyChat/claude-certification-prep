# Five exam tips (from someone who wished they'd been told first)

These are the five judgment habits from the series intro (EP 00). The exam expects judgment, not memorization — nearly every question is a scenario, and these five habits resolve most of them.

## 1. A human stays in the loop for anything that acts

When a scenario involves an output that *does* something — a decision, a customer message, a hiring shortlist, anything high-stakes — the right answer keeps a human approving before it acts. Any option where AI output goes straight to a person's rights, health, money, or livelihood without a qualified reviewer is wrong, however efficient it sounds.

## 2. AI drafts, you decide

Claude is excellent at synthesizing, drafting, and structuring information. It does not make human decisions. Options that delegate the *decision* to Claude (not just the drafting) are traps. Options where Claude produces the input and a person owns the call are usually right.

## 3. Know when to escalate

Not every problem is yours to fix with a better prompt. When a scenario has you rewriting prompts repeatedly, or the need is a system integration, custom tooling, or something beyond configuration — the right answer often escalates to a developer or architect. Recognizing "this is beyond the associate layer" IS the tested skill.

## 4. Eliminate wrong answers first

Work backwards. In almost every question, two options are clearly wrong (one panics, one does nothing). Strike those first, then compare the remaining two — one of which is the trap. The trap usually *feels* diligent, thorough, or responsible; the correct answer is usually cheaper, more scoped, or more boring. When torn, pick the boring one.

## 5. Organization policy and data sensitivity outrank everything

Before anything clever happens, the scenario's data has to be *allowed* into the tool. Personal information, confidential business data, regulated records — org policy decides, and "check the policy / anonymize / escalate to compliance" beats every technically clever option. No prompt technique ever overrides a data rule.
